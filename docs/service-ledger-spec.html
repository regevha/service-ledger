§0

Overview & goals

What this system is for, and why the record matters as much as the instrument.

Technicians servicing a shared fleet of BD flow cytometers — an LSRFortessa running CS&T bead calibration this morning, a FACSAria III getting a jammed sample injector repaired this afternoon, a FACSDiscover S8 due for its quarterly preventive maintenance next week — currently leave that work as paper logs, scanned PDFs in a shared drive, or a spreadsheet someone maintains from memory. None of it is queryable. Nobody can answer "when did the violet-laser detector on the Fortessa last drift out of CV spec" or "what was actually replaced the last time the Aria's injector jammed" without opening a stack of PDFs by hand.

ServiceLedger gives every calibration, repair, and preventive-maintenance event on an instrument a structured, queryable record — captured by scanning the technician's existing printed form or PDF export from BD's own software (FACSDiva / CS&T) and letting Claude's vision capability read it into structured fields. The record is the source of truth for two things: is this instrument currently in spec, and what has been done to it, by whom, and when.

Three goals drive every decision in this document:

  1. Meet the printed form, don't replace it. BD's own software and field technicians already produce a calibration, repair, or PM sheet; the system reads that sheet rather than asking the technician to re-type it into a bespoke screen.
  2. Every report type has a different form, and that's permanent. A CS&T calibration printout, a malfunction/repair report, and a preventive-maintenance checklist share almost no fields, even on the same instrument. The data model treats this as the normal case, not an edge case to special-case around.
  3. Ship the smallest thing that proves the concept. This phase is one evaluator, one instrument family, three report types — two of which resolve to a model-specific variant — and no history to reconstruct. Electron microscopy, other vendors, auditing, versioning, and multi-user roles are all deliberately deferred (§1, §5, §10, §11) until there's a real need driving each one.

Stripped of every implementation detail below, every report the system handles moves through the same three stages:

§1

Users & scope

Who this build is for today, and what's deliberately left out.

The current phase targets a single user evaluating the concept end-to-end: one person acting as both the technician entering data and the reviewer confirming AI-extracted fields. There is no multi-tenant, multi-role, or SSO requirement yet, and — per the scope decision for this phase — no audit trail and no report or template versioning either (see §5 and §10 for what that simplifies). Regulated or compliance-driven requirements (Part 11-style e-signatures, chain of custody, retention limits) are confirmed not relevant to this system at all, not merely deferred — scanned originals are kept indefinitely instead (§10). Two seams are still worth designing in now, because they cost nothing today and are expensive to retrofit:

  • Instrument and report-template records are already multi-instrument, multi-lab shaped (§5) — a location field on instruments costs nothing to add now and everything to backfill later.
  • Auth is abstracted behind a single-user stub (§8) — swapping it for real multi-user auth touches one module, not the whole app.

Instrument scope is narrower still: MVP covers a fixed fleet of three BD flow cytometers — a FACSAria III, an LSRFortessa, and a FACSDiscover S8. Electron microscopy and non-BD cytometry vendors are out of this phase entirely — not stubbed, not scaffolded — and return in Phase 3 (§11) if and when there's a real second fleet to support. Report-type scope covers the three events that make up a BD technician's day-to-day paperwork — calibration, malfunction/repair, and preventive maintenance — not the fuller repertoire (installation qualification, decommissioning) an instrument sees over its whole life. Those rarer report types are a later addition (§11), once these three are in production use. ServiceLedger also stands alone for this phase: there's no planned integration with a LIMS, ELN, or asset-management system, so instrument and report data lives only here, with no external system to reconcile against or export a live feed to.

Design rationale

Everything else — who-changed-what history, edit approval, report versions, a second instrument family — is real engineering effort that would sit unused or untested by a single evaluator with one fleet. Proving the extraction pipeline works well on one well-understood instrument family is worth more right now than spreading the same effort thin across formats from multiple vendors and categories. The seams above keep that narrowing cheap to undo later.

Requirement — no traditional OCR

Reading a scanned form into structured fields must not be built on template- or coordinate-based OCR. This is a hard requirement for this system, fixed regardless of phase or scale, not a default weighed against alternatives — see §4 for why coordinate-based extraction breaks across BD's own model and software variation, and why a vision-capable model reading against a described schema is used instead.

§2

Domain model

The handful of concepts everything else in this document is built from.

  • Instrument — a physical asset: one of the three BD flow cytometers in scope (a FACSAria III, an LSRFortessa, a FACSDiscover S8), identified by serial number, with a fixed instrument_type that stays open for other vendors and categories later.
  • Report template — the expected shape of a report for a given instrument type, report type, and — where it applies — instrument model: what fields exist, their units, and which are required. MVP ships three report types, all on BD flow cytometers: calibration, malfunction/repair, and preventive maintenance. Calibration splits into a CS&T variant (FACSAria III, LSRFortessa) and a spectral variant (FACSDiscover S8). Repair and preventive maintenance both stay a single shared template regardless of model — real BD service reports showed no model-specific structured fields on either (§5). Further report types and schema versioning are both later concerns (§11).
  • Report — one filled-out record: a specific calibration or service event on a specific instrument, on a specific date, with field values conforming (loosely) to a template.
  • Attachment — the scanned printed form or PDF export behind a report.
  • Extraction job — one AI attempt to read an attachment: classifying instrument and report type first, then reading the resolved template into structured fields, with a confidence on every guess along the way.

§3

System architecture

Four moving parts: a browser SPA, an API, a database, and a worker that talks to Claude.

The extraction call to Claude's vision model runs seconds, not milliseconds — long enough that it shouldn't block an HTTP request. A single background worker process, polling a job table in Postgres, is the entire "queue" this needs at single-user scale; it upgrades to Redis/Celery later without changing the API contract, because the API already talks to extraction jobs asynchronously (submit → poll → result).

Why this shape

A separate frontend and backend (rather than a single Next.js app) is worth the extra moving part here specifically because the extraction pipeline is genuinely backend work — file handling, prompt construction, confidence scoring, retries — that benefits from Python's ecosystem (Pillow/pypdf for preprocessing, the Anthropic SDK) and from being testable independent of any UI. FastAPI's automatic OpenAPI schema also gives the React client typed request/response shapes for free via codegen.

§4

AI extraction pipeline

A scanned form becomes structured, confidence-scored fields — then a human confirms before anything is final.

The intake is document-first: the technician doesn't pick an instrument or a report type before uploading — they just upload the scan, and the system figures out both from the document itself. Both the FACSAria III's and the LSRFortessa's model and serial number are typically printed right on a BD service form (e.g. "Installed Product: 647794E6 - LSRFortessa..."), so identifying which instrument this is is usually a confident read. Which report type is the harder call — a real BD Care Work Order carries the same generic layout for a repair, a PM visit, or a calibration, with only a small task-code field distinguishing them — so a wrong guess here is the costliest failure in the whole pipeline: it silently applies the wrong field_schema, asking for fields (baseline_cv_percent on what was actually a repair, say) that don't belong to the document at all. That asymmetry is why classification gets its own step, its own confidence score, and its own mandatory-review treatment, exactly like a low-confidence field does later in the same pipeline — never a silent guess.

  1. Upload. Technician scans the printed form (or uploads a PDF export straight from the instrument's own software) and attaches it to a report shell. This is the only entry path for MVP — there is no blank-form fallback.
  2. Preprocess. Multi-page PDFs are split to pages; images are oriented and downsampled to a size the vision model handles well.
  3. Classify. Claude reads the document against the closed set MVP actually has — three instruments × three report types — and returns an instrument guess, a report-type guess, and a confidence for each. Above threshold, the system resolves the matching template (§5) automatically and moves straight to extraction. Below threshold, extraction pauses and the technician picks instrument and/or report type manually — the old schema-first flow survives as this fallback, not as the default.
  4. Extract. Each page is sent to Claude with the resolved template's field list, units, and expected types, asking for a JSON object of { value, confidence, source_snippet } per field — the snippet lets a reviewer see exactly what printed text the value came from. The prompt asks only for fields the template defines: anything else on the page — a customer account or contract number, a service engineer's email in a header or footer — is never requested and never lands in extracted_fields. source_snippet is likewise cropped tightly around the field itself, not the surrounding free text, so incidental contact or contract details don't enter the database through the review snippet either (§10).
  5. Score. Fields below a confidence threshold (tunable; starts around 0.7) are flagged for mandatory review rather than silently accepted. Printed source text means most fields should land well above this threshold; low readings tend to come from scan quality (glare, low contrast) rather than ambiguous input.
  6. Review. The technician's first checkpoint is confirming what step 3 detected — instrument and report type — before ever looking at a field value; correcting either re-runs extraction against the right template (§6), since every field extracted under a wrong guess is meaningless. Only then does the technician see the source scan side-by-side with the extracted form, flagged fields highlighted first, and accept, correct, or fill in what the model missed.
  7. Commit. On submit, the report moves from extracted to finalized. For MVP this simply writes the final field values to the report — there is no separate audit log of who changed what (§10, §11).
Requirement — why not traditional OCR, even for printed forms

Avoiding traditional OCR is a hard requirement for this system (§1), not a preference weighed against alternatives. Printed source text removes the hardest OCR problem, but not the layout problem: BD's own CS&T and FACSDiva software print a fairly consistent report, but not an identical one — a FACSAria III's calibration report has a different laser/detector table than an LSRFortessa's, and the FACSDiscover S8's spectral workflow prints a differently-shaped report again, with layout drifting further across software versions. Template/coordinate-based OCR is brittle to that variation — it re-breaks with every instrument model or software update. A vision-capable model reading against a described schema, rather than fixed coordinates, tolerates that drift and still returns a confidence score per field, which a regex- or coordinate-based OCR pipeline doesn't give you at all.

§5

Data model

Structured columns for what's always true of a report; a flexible schema for what's true only of one report type.

The central design decision: instruments, reports, and report_templates are real relational tables with real columns, but a report's instrument-specific readings live in a single extracted_fields JSONB column, validated at write-time against its template's field_schema. A new per-detector reading, or a whole new report type down the line, never needs its own migration — only its template does.

Design rationale — why JSONB, not one wide table

Three kinds of variation push toward JSONB rather than fixed columns. Across report types, a calibration's baseline_cv_percent, a repair's work_performed, and a PM's calibrated_tools have nothing in common — a single reports table with a column for every field across all three would be mostly null on any given row. Within a single report type, calibration still varies by instrument model — a FACSDiscover S8's spectral calibration fields don't exist on the other two models' CS&T rows; repair and preventive maintenance both turned out not to need this axis at all, once checked against real service reports (§5). And within a single template, some fields are themselves per-detector, with a count that varies sharply across the fleet — a conventional multi-PMT FACSAria III versus a FACSDiscover S8, whose spectral detection array runs many more channels. A JSONB field validated against its resolved template's field_schema absorbs all three kinds of variation without a wider-and-wider table of nullable, model-specific columns.

§6

Review & correction

Every report starts from a scan for MVP — the generated form exists to review and correct it, not to fill it from scratch.

A report's template field_schema drives a generated review form on the frontend, so a new instrument type or a changed field list is a template edit, not a frontend release. Two flows converge on the same report record, and both require an attachment — there is no blank-form, no-scan path in this phase:

  • Scan, then confirm — the extraction flow in §4, ending with the technician reviewing pre-filled values.
  • Scan, then fill the gaps — extraction handles most fields; anything the model couldn't read (a smudge, a field the scan cut off) is left blank and highlighted for manual entry rather than blocking the whole report.

Before either flow reaches a single field, there's a checkpoint neither had to worry about under the old schema-first intake: confirming what step 3 of §4 classified. The review screen opens with the detected instrument and report type shown as a normal pre-filled, editable pair — high-confidence classifications look exactly like a high-confidence field elsewhere on the form. Accepting them moves straight into field review. Changing either one re-runs extraction against the newly resolved template before showing any fields, since values extracted under the wrong template aren't corrections to make, they're noise to discard.

Editing a finalized report in this phase overwrites its fields in place — there is no version history, so a correction replaces the previous value rather than sitting alongside it. That tradeoff is acceptable for a single evaluator; §10 and §11 cover when it stops being acceptable and what replaces it.

§7

Reporting & analytics

The payoff for structuring the data: questions that were previously "go open forty PDFs."

  • Instrument timeline — shipped. The instrument detail page's trend chart plots any numeric field across an instrument's finalized report history (e.g. baseline_cv_percent per detector over its last several calibrations), with a legend and hover tooltip for a per-detector/per-laser field and a single line for a flat one. Unlike the model-specific field list originally planned here, the chartable field list isn't hardcoded per model at all: GET /instruments/{id}/trend-fields derives it from whatever numeric fields that specific instrument's own finalized reports actually resolved to, so a FACSAria III only ever offers baseline_cv_percent/fluidics_pressure_psi-style calibration fields because that's what its reports carry, a new template's numeric fields show up automatically, and nothing fleet-wide ever mixes one model's fields with another's.
  • Fleet dashboard — partially shipped. The Analytics tab (GET /analytics/fleet) covers the roll-up half: total labor hours, units replaced, most-replaced parts, labor hours by instrument and by fault category, and pass/fail rate by report type — all computed live from finalized reports, nothing cached. Per-instrument "days since last calibration" / "currently out of spec" / upcoming next_service_due status-at-a-glance is not built yet and stays a real gap.
  • Filtered search — shipped. The Reports tab filters by instrument, report type, status, date range (date_from/date_to, both real columns per §5), and technician — the last a case-insensitive partial match (ILIKE) against technician_name rather than equality, since it's free text typed at intake (§4) rather than a fixed dropdown value, and debounced client-side so it doesn't re-query on every keystroke. Filtering on a specific field value inside extracted_fields (e.g. "every calibration where baseline_cv_percent exceeds 5%") is not built.
  • Exports — CSV and per-report PDF both shipped. GET /reports/export exports the active filtered view as CSV; GET /reports/{id}/pdf renders one report as a formatted PDF for sharing outside the system. The PDF isn't a second, independently-maintained layout — it walks the same resolved template's field_schema in the same order the review screen does, so it can never show a technician something they didn't actually see and finalize on screen. Available on any report with a resolved template, not gated on status=finalized — same as the review screen itself has no such gate.
  • Structured template & instrument management — shipped, not originally scoped here. A CRUD editor for report_templates (create/edit/delete a template's field schema, including enum options and object[] item schemas) and for the instrument fleet (create/edit every field but instrument_type, via PATCH /instruments/{id}) both shipped as part of Phase 1 (§11) — ahead of §10's audit/versioning boundary, since neither carries edit history, matching the single-evaluator tradeoff §10 already accepts.
  • Natural-language query (phase 2+, unbuilt) — a question like "which instruments needed more than one repair in the last six months" translated by Claude into a filter against the reporting API, not a raw SQL pass-through, so it can't touch data outside the same permission boundary a normal query would.
Design rationale

Trend, drift, and roll-up reporting all work the same way because extracted_fields is JSONB with known keys per template, not free text — Postgres can index and aggregate into extracted_fields->>'baseline_cv_percent' directly. This is the payoff for insisting on template-validated extraction back in §4 rather than accepting whatever shape the model returns, and it's also why the trend chart's field list above can be derived rather than hardcoded — the same JSONB keys that back a chart are just as queryable for "what fields does this instrument actually have."

§10

Non-functional considerations

Data sensitivity

Scanned service logs can incidentally carry more than instrument state — a customer account or contract number, a service engineer's email address, sample IDs or project names visible on the same page. None of that is wanted, and none of it is collected: no template's field_schema defines a contact, billing, or contract field, the extraction prompt asks only for fields the template defines (§4), and the source_snippet kept per field is cropped tightly around that field rather than the surrounding text. That data may still exist on the scanned original itself — the raw attachment isn't redacted, only what gets parsed out of it — so object storage access should be scoped as tightly as auth allows even at single-user scale, and attachments should never be sent anywhere except the extraction call itself.

Auditing and versioning — deliberately out of scope for now

MVP has no record of who changed a field, when, or what it used to say, and no history of a report or template across edits — a correction simply overwrites the previous value. That's a real tradeoff, not an oversight: with one evaluator there's no second party to audit against, so the tradeoff costs nothing yet. It stops being free the moment a second technician can quietly change another's report, or a template changes underneath reports that already reference it — at that point, an append-only edit log and versioned templates (§11, Phase 2) become necessary rather than nice-to-have.

Scalability path

Nothing here assumes single-user forever: instruments.location and the auth stub (§1) are the two seams that carry this design from one evaluator toward a multi-technician deployment without a schema rewrite. Audit logging and versioning are additive tables when they're built (§11) — they don't require reshaping reports or report_templates as they exist today.

Retention: indefinite, by decision

MVP has no delete path for attachments — no endpoint, no retention job, nothing prunes old scans — and that's now a confirmed design decision rather than a placeholder to revisit: scanned originals are retained indefinitely, and regulated or compliance-driven use (Part 11, chain-of-custody, right-to-erasure) is confirmed not relevant to this system. Unlike auditing and versioning (above), retention and deletion aren't on the roadmap for any later phase — if that ever changes, it would be a different system, not a future phase of this one.

Decisions that shape Phase 1 but are yours to make, not architectural defaults to assume.

  • Resolved: classify and extract shipped as two separate model calls against two schemas, matching the description in §4 rather than a combined single-prompt approach — app/services/classification.py and app/services/extraction.py are distinct modules, each with its own stub/live-Claude switch.
  • Resolved: the classification confidence threshold does not reuse the field-level default — config.py sets classification_confidence_threshold = 0.85 separately from field_confidence_threshold = 0.7, reflecting that a wrong report-type guess is a costlier mistake than one wrong field.
